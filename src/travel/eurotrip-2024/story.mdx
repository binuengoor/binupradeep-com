<Chapter name="rome" top>

<p class="b-lede b-dropcap">First time in Europe, and I had a movie playing in my head. You know the one. Golden light, cobbled lanes, a violin somewhere, every street beautiful, a little dreamy, perfectly in order. I live in American cities, which are big and tidy, and I'd always figured America borrowed its good looks from the Europeans who built it. So I was going to the <em>original</em>. The director's cut. Full-strength charm.</p>

That movie lasted about forty minutes in Rome. The real thing turned out to be **better**, because it was alive, and loud, and a little bit mad, and weirdly familiar. More on that shortly.

I'm also the kind of person who plans a holiday down to the platform number. For this one I set 1 AM and 2 AM alarms for ticket releases, hunted award seats like a man possessed, and built a day-by-day plan with backup plans for the backup plans. So this post comes in two flavors: what these places actually feel like when you're standing in them, and the "if you go" notes I wish someone had slipped into my pocket.

<Note icon="heart" label="Meet Miss Vibes">Arsha. Low maintenance, high enthusiasm, plan-B-proof.</Note>

And my travel partner: Arsha, my wife, the most low-maintenance, high-enthusiasm co-traveller a planner could dream of. Her entire travel philosophy fits on a postcard: *keep it cozy, keep the snacks coming, Binu will sort it out.* When a plan collapses, she doesn't panic. She gives me one calm, trusting little look that says "Plan B, chief, whenever you're ready," and waits for the rabbit to come out of the hat. (So far, the rabbit has always come out. The pressure is adorable.)

Our division of labor is very clear. I bring the planning, the meticulous calculations, the weather forecasts, the tickets, the organization and the communication. She brings **the vibes.** (Which is why, in this story, she goes by **Miss Vibes**.) And a trip needs both.

<blockquote class="b-pull"><p>All planning and no vibes is just a very efficient spreadsheet.</p></blockquote>

All vibes and no planning is a lovely story about missing your train. Together, we get a trip like this one.

Her one weakness: she can be sweet-talked. "It's just a small walk" has taken her up a *surprising* number of mountains. And once she's up there, she becomes the happiest person on the trail. Keep an eye on that, because it comes back in a big way.

We did the whole trip without a rental car. If it had rails, a cable or a propeller, we rode it: high-speed trains, regional trains, the Metro, electric bikes, city buses, postbuses, cable cars, gondolas, a cogwheel train and a lake boat. Eleven days, and I never once had to find parking.

And the flight in had a bonus: the route goes right over Switzerland, so Arsha held her phone to the window and filmed the Alps, white and enormous and *ridiculously* close. Then **Milano Centrale**. Take a station, add the ambition of a cathedral, and you've got it. It's a terminal station, so all the lines just stop at the end of the hall, with trains lined up like they're waiting for a race. We never even stepped outside Milan. We hopped on a red **Italo** high-speed train and flew at about 250 km/h straight to Rome's Termini. A red bullet. Arsha was smiling out of the window like a kid on her first train.

<p class="b-turn">And then Rome.</p>

I stepped out expecting the movie. What I got:

<blockquote class="b-pull"><p>A city that looks the part and behaves like India.</p></blockquote>

Make no mistake, it's gorgeous. But it's gorgeous *with elbows*. People walk like they have somewhere important to be and you're slightly in the way. No "excuse me," no personal space bubble, just a friendly squeeze. Pickpocket warnings everywhere, so I walked with one hand on my bag and a big grin on my face. And the cars! A zebra crossing in Rome isn't a promise, it's a suggestion. If they have to go through you, they will.

My theory: when a city has had a few thousand years of history, it has also had a few thousand years of entropy, and entropy's love led to chaos. But... I loved it. It felt strangely like home, as if it were wearing a very expensive Italian jacket.

<Polaroid name="d1-1806-IMG_0937" alt="Arsha facing the folding wooden doors of a very old, very small elevator" caption="the polite wooden cage" tilt={-3} float="l" />

We rolled into Roma Termini a little after 5:20 PM, and I pressed my face to the window like the tourist I was. A short Metro ride from Termini got us to our stay near Porta San Giovanni, on a street that looked like every Roman postcard minus the filter. The building had an elevator I can only describe as a polite wooden cage: folding doors, room for two if one of them agrees not to breathe, and a rattle that says *I have seen things.* Arsha eyed it like a ride at a fair.

The room was bright and tidy, with a window onto the neighborhood. We allowed ourselves a few minutes of "let's just sit for a second," and then Rome called.

<div class="b-clear"></div>

<Polaroid name="d1-1956-IMG_0945" alt="A shell-shaped sfogliatella pastry with one bite missing, held up on a Roman street" caption="Exhibit A: the crime scene" tilt={2.5} float="r" />

We dropped our bags, freshened up, and hit the streets. First stop: a bakery, and our first Roman snack: a **sfogliatella riccia**, the iconic shell-shaped pastry from Naples, a hundred paper-thin layers of crisp pastry folded into a clam shell around sweet ricotta. It looked like a work of art. It tasted like a lesson.

<p class="b-handline">Lesson: never judge a pastry by its looks. When in doubt, Google.</p>

<div class="b-clear"></div>

Before the Colosseum, though, there was a walk I'd nearly left out of the story. From the hotel we went through the old Aurelian Wall at Porta San Giovanni, a gate people have been strolling through for about 1,700 years, and straight into the huge square of San Giovanni in Laterano: an ochre palace, a basilica with a skyline of saints on its roof, and in the middle of it all an Egyptian obelisk, the tallest standing in Rome and more than 3,000 years old. It was just standing there in the evening light, as if nobody had told it that it was in the wrong country.

<Postcard name="d1-1946-IMG_0941" alt="The Aurelian Wall and the gate of Porta San Giovanni across a wide Roman crossing in evening light" stamp={['IT', 'ROMA']} postmark="ROMA|24 MAG|2024" hand="Porta San Giovanni. 1,700 years of people walking through." />

Then came a stretch of ancient wall and brick arches, where Arsha did her "is this normal?" face. (In Rome, yes. Entirely normal.) That's the thing about this city: the best moments weren't on my spreadsheet.

Then we just walked. Rome is made of piazzas, wide open squares around every other corner, so we drifted from one to the next with our mouths slightly open, and then climbed **Aventine Hill**. On the way up, the Colosseum waves at you from the distance. You pass the **Circus Maximus**, where Romans once watched chariot races, which is basically a huge field now, but you can *feel* the noise that used to be there. Arsha posed in front of it.

<Fig name="d1-2026-IMG_0954" variant="bleed" alt="Arsha walking along the edge of the Circus Maximus at sunset, the ruins of the Palatine glowing behind her" chip="20:26 · Circus Maximus" />

I tried to capture it on my phone, and it came out dark and soft, partly because people were waiting behind me, and partly because I don't think it <em>can</em> be captured. We arrived around sunset. The queue was only six to eight people: a few couples, some older folks, one family, everyone politely waiting to peek through a hole in a door, which is a very special kind of happy crowd. The walk up had been a bit longer than I'd planned for, but on vacation, I'll walk anywhere, and so will Arsha.

Then, across the Tiber to **Trastevere**, whose name literally means "beyond the Tevere." It used to be the working-class side of town and is now Rome's food playground: narrow lanes, buzzing little places, good vibes in the air. We started with a **Trapizzino** (pizza dough folded into a pocket and stuffed with something delicious), and then came the moment that changed my life.

<Note icon="leaf" label="Back home in Kerala">Trastevere has a very Fort Kochi feel.</Note>

<div class="b-clear"></div>

<p class="b-bigword">Supplì.</p>

A fried rice ball, crunchy on the outside, a core of melted cheese inside. It looks like a cutlet. Everyone says it's Rome's parippuvada, and it's... not at all like parippuvada, but I understand the instinct. I ate it, closed my eyes, and quietly decided it was my favorite food in Rome. (Spoiler: it stayed that way for the whole trip. We hunted down supplì at a ridiculous number of places over the next few days. Some had pasta inside, and those are also dangerous.)

After dinner, the bus never came. We waited. We waited some more. In the end we rented **electric bikes through Uber**, barely pedaling, the motor doing the work, and rode home through Rome at night, right past the front of the Colosseum, lit up like it was waiting just for us. I filmed it. I'm pretty sure I was laughing the whole ride. A missing bus turned into one of the best rides of the trip.

And that was Day 1. Technically it was "Day 0," but who's counting? (Me. I'm counting. I have a spreadsheet.) Our legs were done, and the real walking marathon was about to begin tomorrow.

<Ticket day={2} route="Sab 25 Mag 2024 · San Giovanni → Ottaviano · Città del Vaticano" short="25 Mag · Vaticano" title="Popes, Pillars and a Malayali Auntie" when="Sat, May 25 · Vatican City" stamp={['SCV', 'VATICANO']} />

<p class="b-lede b-dropcap">You walk into Vatican City, an entire country inside a city, and the first thing you learn is how small you are.</p>

<Polaroid name="d2-0713-IMG_0984" alt="Arsha on an empty Metro platform early in the morning" caption="awake, mostly" tilt={-2.5} float="r" />

We did it the sensible way: Metro Line A from San Giovanni, leaving around 7 AM, with Arsha doing her best "I am awake, I promise" pose on the platform. The reward for an early start was St. Peter's Square at a quarter to eight, long morning shadows across the cobbles and almost nobody in it. By ten the square would fill up with tour groups. At 7:48 there was just us and a very large colonnade.

**St. Peter's Basilica** doesn't need a ticket, which is why every tour group on earth is there. We went early, and it paid off: hardly a queue. On the way, we grabbed cappuccinos to go from an Italian café, and I understood the hype with the first sip. The shot, the foam, the proportions, all perfect, and you drink it walking around like a local. Best coffee-and-stroll combination.

<div class="b-clear"></div>

Inside, the basilica plays a trick on you. It looks like a decently large, very elegant church, until you see a human standing next to a pillar. I stood Arsha at the base of one for scale. She practically vanished. The pillar is the size of a small mountain.

And you can't visit this place without climbing the **dome**. About 800 steps. Halfway up, the stairs let you out onto an inside balcony right at the base of the dome, high above the altar, and the gold-and-blue Latin lettering around the dome, which looks like normal writing from the ground, is made of letters taller than a grown adult.

Then you keep going into the gap between the dome's two shells. The walls lean in, the passage narrows, and you go single file, a little hot and a lot excited. (Pro tip: do not do this with a giant backpack. Travel light, always.) Then you pop out on the rooftop terrace and, ta-da, all of Rome is at your feet. Worth every one of those 800 steps.

One more secret from St. Peter's: most of those giant "paintings" aren't paintings. They're **mosaics**, made of millions of tiny colored pieces. From close you can see the tiles. Step back and it's an oil painting.

And then, behind glass in a side chapel, the one sculpture that stopped me in my tracks: **Michelangelo's Pietà**. He carved it when he was about 24, and it's the only piece he ever signed (look for the name on the sash across Mary's chest). My brain kept insisting it was soft: the folds of the robe, the weight of the arm, the calm on her face. It is marble.

<p class="b-handline">It is marble that has forgotten it is marble.</p>

By about 11 we were at the **Vatican Museums**, which started life as the Popes' private art collection. Imagine being able to hire Michelangelo and Raphael. We took an audio guide, because otherwise you'd wander for hours with a happy, lost face. Highlights: the **Laocoön** statue (you can see veins in the marble, in *marble*), the Gallery of Maps, tapestries that look like photographs, and the Raphael Rooms, painted floor to ceiling.

And then, the **Sistine Chapel**. No photos allowed, which, honestly, helps. After all those rooms of art, you step in, look up, and your brain just says "okay, wow." It's one thing to see it in books, another to stand under it. The thought that kept coming back: this was made *hundreds of years ago*. Where I'm from, a 100-year-old building is ancient. Here I'm looking at something that's older than the idea of my country. The skill, the scale, the sheer nerve of it, mind-blowing.

<blockquote class="b-pull"><p>Michelangelo reportedly saw himself as a sculptor, and the Pope more or less talked him into the ceiling. Thank you, Pope.</p></blockquote>

<Polaroid name="d2-1347-IMG_1247" alt="Two blackboards listing pinsa and pizza by the slice at Bonci's Pizzarium" caption="the blackboard that decided lunch" tilt={2} float="r" />

After the Sistine Chapel, lunch was a short walk from the exit: **Bonci's Pizzarium**, a pizza-by-the-slice counter with a blackboard of Roman pinsa and pizza al taglio, the kind of place where you point, they cut, and everyone eats standing up. (Ask me what we ordered and I'll draw a blank; whatever it was, it vanished.) Best lunch for a man whose feet had just walked through the Vatican.

The afternoon was for wandering (with a gelato in each hand, naturally): Piazza Navona with Bernini's fountain, a walk past the Pantheon from the outside (no tickets that afternoon, so we saved it for tomorrow), and the Spanish Steps, packed with happy people.

She turned, laughed, and we said hello. That's how we met **Celine Auntie**, who has lived in Rome for 24 years. When we said we're from Thrissur, her whole face lit up. *Her family home is right there, near Pazhuvil.* Two strangers in a foreign city, from the same corner of Kerala, standing in front of the wine. We swapped numbers, and she promised that if anyone we know visits Rome and needs tips or help, to reach out to her. Malayalis really are in every corner of the world, and I'll never get tired of that.

Dinner was a neighborhood trattoria, **Trattoria Re di Roma**. We had both changed into black for dinner without discussing it, which is the kind of thing that happens when two people have shared a life for a while. There was a starter of grilled aubergine under a pile of fresh tomato, a carafe of red wine, and then Arsha's **amatriciana** and my **carbonara**, with crispy guanciale and the confidence of a dish that has been perfect for centuries. Both were rich, comforting and gone embarrassingly fast.

And that was Day 2: popes, pillars and one very welcome piece of home. Tomorrow was the day I'd set the alarms for.

<Ticket day={3} route="Dom 26 Mag 2024 · Colosseo · Foro Romano · Pantheon" short="26 Mag · Colosseo" title="Bread, Circuses and 25,000 Steps" when="Sun, May 26 · Ancient Rome" stamp={['ROMA', 'COLOSSEO']} />

<p class="b-lede b-dropcap">This was the day I planned the whole trip around.</p>

When we started planning Italy, two things were non-negotiable: the Vatican Museums and the **Colosseum**. And the Colosseum is the toughest ticket in town. They release tickets exactly 30 days ahead, hour by hour, so I was awake at 1 AM, then 2 AM, refreshing the page like it owed me money, racing the bots. The special underground tour vanished in the blink of an eye. But I snagged regular morning entry at about 9:30 and punched the air in my kitchen. If you don't book ahead, you either stand in line for hours or pay a scalper a ridiculous markup.

Breakfast first, Italian style: light, sweet, no savory spread. Cappuccino plus a **cornetto**, the Italian croissant. I had mine filled with sweet cream. Arsha went plain, like a professional.

<Phone clip="cyclists" float="r" alt="A pack of racing cyclists whizzing past the Colosseum" caption="the welcome parade" />

The sky was blazing blue when we reached the **Colosseum**, no clouds at all, the opposite of yesterday's grey. Audio guide in ears, we walked in. (Bonus: a pack of cyclists came whizzing past the Colosseum just as we arrived, and I filmed the whole parade.)

Honestly, I hadn't expected much. I knew it'd be huge and iconic, tick the box. But the *mechanics* of it blew my mind. Over 50,000 people could sit there, with 80 exits so the crowd could flow out in minutes. Under the arena floor was a whole hidden maze of corridors, cells and lifts worked by ropes and pulleys, where lions, leopards and even giraffes (from Africa!) were launched up through trapdoors into the arena. No radios. No electronics. Just thousands of people who all knew exactly what to do, at exactly the right time.

<div class="b-clear"></div>

<blockquote class="b-pull"><p>As a project manager, I stood there with the biggest, nerdiest grin on my face thinking: this is the best-run live event in history.</p></blockquote>

<Fig name="d3-1100-IMG_1463" variant="bleed" alt="Inside the Colosseum: the exposed maze of walls and corridors under the old arena floor, the tiered stands rising behind" chip="11:00 · The hidden maze" />

And then the other feeling: the weight. Walking on the arena floor, you can't help thinking about everything that happened there, and how much blood was spilled on that ground. Rulers kept the public happy with "bread and circuses": free food and non-stop spectacle. The outer wall is half gone now, and the little square holes all over the stone are where people dug out iron clamps for scrap. What's standing today is the skeleton of something that must have looked unbelievable in gleaming white.

<Postcard name="d3-1136-IMG_1538" alt="Arsha and Binu in sun hats and sunglasses taking a selfie in front of the Colosseum under a clear blue sky" stamp={['IT', 'COLOSSEO']} postmark="ROMA|26 MAG|2024" hand="Sun hats on, not a cloud in sight." />

Right across from it: the **Roman Forum**, the civic heart of ancient Rome. You wander past the Senate House, old courts and markets, and the spot where Julius Caesar was cremated. Under the midday sun it was gorgeous and absolutely exhausting.

Two Forum landmarks deserve a name: the **Arch of Titus**, nearly 2,000 years old and still doing its job as a gateway, and the enormous arches of the **Basilica of Maxentius**, which look like someone built a cathedral and then forgot the roof. We climbed **Palatine Hill** too, where Rome's emperors built their palaces (the word "palace" comes from the hill's name), and from the top you look down on the Forum and the Circus Maximus we'd seen the night before.

Revived, on to the **Pantheon**. Two thousand years old and still standing, because someone had the sense to turn it into a church early on. Next, the **Trevi Fountain**, packed with people, but we squeezed right up to the edge. Coin in the right hand, thrown over the left shoulder, so you come back to Rome. Fun fact: Rome's fountains run on gravity, fed by ancient aqueducts, and the public ones, called *nasoni*, give you free, ice-cold drinking water on every street.

Rome's cobblestones (*sanpietrini*) are no joke, and our feet complained loudly. We picked up more supplì (obviously), took them home, and called it a night, completely wrecked and completely happy.

<section class="b-closing">

## What I'd tell you over chai

- **Rome** looks like a movie and behaves like India. Walk everywhere, eat supplì, book the Colosseum and Vatican a month ahead, and hold your bag.
- **Florence** is the exhale. One evening, one hill, one sandwich.
- **Switzerland** is where the trip turns unreal. Pick your peak by the weather, take the walk your travel partner doesn't want to take, and skip the "must-do" if it means a better day.
- **Never trust a 40-minute connection.**

</section>

What surprised me? Rome. What disappointed me? Honestly, nothing. I wouldn't change a thing, except to add days. Next time: **Pompeii**, **Venice**, and maybe the **Dolomites**, a landscape I know almost nothing about, which is exactly why I want to see it.

If I had to boil eleven days down to one idea, it's that planning and vibes need each other. The spreadsheet got us the Colosseum at 9:58 and the Vatican Museums at 11. Miss Vibes got us a perfect Roman wall, a pistachio gelato on a station platform, and the courage to turn a late train into a lake view.

<blockquote class="b-pull"><p>The plan gave us the skeleton; the detours gave us the story.</p></blockquote>

Italy gave us history, art and pasta. Switzerland gave us beauty and outdoor infrastructure that felt almost too perfect to be real. And somewhere between a keyhole in Rome and a waterfall in Gimmelwald, a planner and a reluctant hiker had the best trip of their lives.
